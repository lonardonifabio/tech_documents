The Use of Large Language Models in the Legal Sector

Authors: Olivier Sib-ony, Cass R. Sunstein

This document explores the rapidly increasing adoption of Large Language Models (LLMs) within the legal profession globally. Initially, the use of LLMs was primarily observed among judges, judicial support staff, prosecutors, and lawyers, who began leveraging these tools for drafting legal documents, formulating judicial decisions, and constructing arguments during court proceedings. The applications of LLMs are remarkably diverse, spanning constitutional, administrative, privacy, criminal, and family law areas. Several countries, including Argentina, Australia, Brazil, Canada, South Africa, Spain, and the United States, have witnessed judges and lawyers utilizing LLMs for tasks such as summarizing judicial decisions in accessible language, conducting legal research, supporting litigation efforts, adding context to legal cases, summarizing legal texts, and drafting correspondence like emails and contracts. Notably, the tools utilized extend beyond general-purpose commercial LLMs to include specialized legal LLMs and open-source alternatives. However, the document highlights significant concerns regarding the misuse of these technologies. Reports have emerged detailing instances where judges and lawyers have inadvertently produced judicial decisions or submitted legal documents containing fabricated references to non-existent rulings, directly attributable to the flawed outputs of AI chatbots. This underscores the critical need for careful oversight, validation, and responsible implementation of LLMs within the legal system. The document cites various sources including Adams (2023), Ambrogi (2023), Benetts et al. (2023), CEPEJ (2024), Gutiér-rez (2024), Harden (2024), LexisNexis (2023), Merken (2025), and Morcillo (…), indicating a comprehensive review of the current landscape and associated risks. The increasing reliance on LLMs presents both opportunities for efficiency and potential pitfalls that must be addressed proactively to maintain the integrity and reliability of the legal process. Further research and development are needed to mitigate these risks and ensure the responsible integration of AI into the legal domain.

  • The Guidelines aim to advance universal respect for justice and the rule of law while ensuring the protection and promotion of human rights and fundamental freedoms.
  • The Guidelines offer principles and specific recommendations for judicial organisations and individuals implementing AI systems, including generative AI, to ensure an ethical and human rights-respecting manner.
  • The Guidelines develop 15 principles focused on protection of human rights, proportionality, feasibility, safety, and transparency within the AI system lifecycle.
