China’s Drive Toward Self-Reliance in Artificial Intelligence

Authors: Wendy Chang, Rebecca Arcesati, Antonia Hmaidi

This MERICS Report, published in July 2025, analyzes China’s strategic push for self-reliance within the field of Artificial Intelligence. The report, produced by the China Tech Observatory (CTO) of Mercator Institute for China Studies (MERICS) – funded by the German Ministry for Research, Technology and Space (BMFTR) – provides a comprehensive assessment of China’s advancements and ambitions in this critical technology area. The core finding is that China is aggressively pursuing self-sufficiency in AI across all technological layers, viewing it as paramount for both national security and economic stability. This drive is largely a response to US technology export controls, with Beijing explicitly framing the pursuit of "independent and controllable" AI as a key objective. The report details a layered approach, with the heaviest state investment concentrated in the capital-intensive semiconductor sector, reflecting the strategic importance of hardware. Furthermore, software frameworks are being developed through the efforts of major technology companies, demonstrating a decentralized strategy. The highest levels of AI development – specifically, large language models and their applications – are supported by an enabling environment, though receive comparatively less direct state intervention. The report highlights the potential for valuable insights for Europe as it navigates its own digital sovereignty initiatives, suggesting that China’s approach offers a model for achieving technological independence. Ultimately, the document underscores the significant geopolitical implications of China’s AI ambitions and the challenges posed by its rapid technological development. The research project’s three-year timeline indicates a sustained effort to monitor and understand China’s evolving technological landscape.

Key Concepts:

  • China's pursuit of self-reliance in AI for national and economic security.
  • Beijing's objective of 'independent and controllable' AI.
  • State support for semiconductors and Big Tech in AI development.
  • China's efforts as a learning opportunity for Europe's digital sovereignty.
  • Indigenization of EDA software – efforts to adapt and utilize domestic software solutions.
  • Huawei's leadership in the semiconductor industry – Huawei's dominant role in coordinating chip manufacturing and accessing manufacturing capacity.
  • Manufacturing bottlenecks – Limitations in chip production capacity and availability of key components like HBM.
  • US export controls – Restrictions on Huawei's access to advanced semiconductor technology.
  • Shift in LLM development: Several Chinese LLM companies (e.g., Yi Once, Baichuan) have pivoted away from building foundational LLMs and towards applications.
  • Open-source ecosystem: The Chinese government's support for open-source software development has facilitated rapid LLM development.
