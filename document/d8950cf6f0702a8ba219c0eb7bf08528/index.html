LLM Classification of Legal and Governance Documents - AI Risk Taxonomy

Authors: MIT (airisk), Unknown

This document details a research project conducted by MIT's airisk team investigating the application of Large Language Models (LLMs) for classifying legal and governance documents related to AI risks. The core of the study involved evaluating the performance of several LLMs – Claude Sonnet 4.5, Claude Opus 4.1, and GPT-5 – alongside human reviewers, specifically focusing on documents sourced from the ETO AGORA archive. The team initially tested a comparative approach across six documents, assessing the agreement between LLM classifications and human consensus. Subsequently, they scaled up their analysis, utilizing Claude Sonnet 4.5 to classify over 950 governance documents based on a defined taxonomy encompassing AI risks, mitigation strategies, and associated governance concepts. The findings revealed a strong performance from Claude Sonnet 4.5 and Claude Opus 4.1, demonstrating comparable or superior agreement with human reviewers when classifying the initial six documents. Notably, the most frequently identified risk domains were Governance failure, AI system security vulnerabilities & attacks, and Lack of transparency or interpretability, aligning with a significant portion of the ETO AGORA dataset. Conversely, areas like AI Welfare and Rights, Multi-agent risks, and Economic and cultural devaluation of human effort received comparatively less attention. Sectoral analysis highlighted Public Administration (excluding National Security), Scientific R&D, and National Security as the most heavily covered areas, while sectors such as Accommodation, Food, and Other Services, Arts, Entertainment, and Recreation, and Real Estate and Rental and Leasing were significantly underrepresented in the classification efforts. The research team is currently seeking feedback and expressions of interest in further collaboration. This project represents an early exploration into leveraging LLMs for automated risk assessment and governance within the rapidly evolving landscape of AI regulation and oversight. The methodology employed and the resulting insights offer a valuable starting point for developing more robust and comprehensive AI risk taxonomies and classification systems. Further research is needed to refine the LLM models and expand the scope of the analysis to include a broader range of sectors and risk categories.
